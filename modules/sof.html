Filtering along structure: cleaning the data without flattening it

Averaging suppresses random noise. Averaging in the wrong direction also suppresses the reflectors. This module builds a noisy section, lets you run five different filters over it, and shows what each one keeps and what it throws away. It starts with the one decision that matters more than the choice of filter: which direction to average in.

1Along the bed, or across it

The same mean filter, pointed two different ways — the filter selector is inert here

A filter that averages neighboring traces has to decide where on those traces to read. Read at the same time sample on every trace and you are averaging horizontally, which is only correct where the reflector happens to be flat. Read at a time that shifts with the dip and you are averaging along the bed, which is correct everywhere.

Both panels below use exactly the same mean filter, on the clean version of the model. Three of the header controls are therefore inert in this step, on purpose. The noise sliders do nothing, because with no noise in the picture anything the filter removes is signal. The filter selector does nothing either, because both panels run the mean whatever it says, so that the only difference between them is which direction the window points. Every header control takes effect again from step 2 onward, and step 3 is where the choice of filter becomes the subject.

2 Averaged along dip window follows the bed

On the flat part of the section the two are almost identical. Wherever the beds dip, on the flanks of the fold and everywhere below the fault, the horizontal filter is averaging across the wavelet instead of along it, so it cancels part of the reflection. That is not noise suppression. That is throwing away signal, and the steeper the dip the more of it goes.

What this means—

Signal loss is the drop in RMS amplitude against the same region of the clean model, quoted as a percentage of that model's RMS amplitude there. It is measured only where the beds dip by more than about half a sample per trace. A number near zero means the filter left the reflection alone. Raise the fault throw to give it something steep to work with.

2What is actually in the window

Every filter here sees this same list of numbers

Move the analysis point around the section and watch the list on the right. Those are the values the window collects, gathered along the local dip. A filter is nothing more than a rule for turning that list into one number to write back at the center.

Sorted, the list makes the differences between the filters obvious. Random noise spreads the values out evenly. A bad sample puts one value a long way from the rest, at one end of the sorted list — which is exactly where a rule that trims the ends will find it.

3Four rules, one window

Mean, alpha-trimmed, median, LUM

All four use the single dipping plane through the analysis point — one value per trace, nothing above or below. They differ only in how much of that list they are willing to discard.

  • Mean. Average everything. Best at suppressing random noise, worst at surviving a bad sample, because one wild value drags the average with it.
  • Alpha-trimmed mean. Sort, throw away a fraction α of the list from each end, average the rest. At α = 0 it is the mean; at α = 0.5 it is the median. The useful settings are in between, where a spike is discarded but the averaging that suppresses random noise is kept.
  • Median. Take the middle value. Immune to spikes, but it is one sample rather than an average, so it does less about random noise.
  • LUM. Keep the value that is already there unless it falls outside the trimmed range, and only then pull it back to the edge of that range. It does nothing at all to a sample the window agrees with, which is what keeps it sharp.

4The principal component filter

The only one that uses the whole 3D window

The four filters in step 3 look at one value per trace. This one looks at the whole window, every trace and every sample in the vertical half-window. It asks a different question. What single waveform, scaled differently on each trace, best explains all of them at once?

That waveform is the first eigenvector of the covariance matrix between the traces. Each trace is then rebuilt as its own projection onto it. Anything the traces share survives; anything only one trace has does not, because it cannot be written as a scaled copy of a shared waveform.

1 The window, trace by trace gray in, crimson out

2 How much of the window is one waveform eigenvalue spectrum

The bar chart is the energy explained by each successive eigenvector. Where the traces are coherent the first bar takes nearly everything, and keeping only it loses almost no signal. Where they are not — at the fault, or with the noise turned up — the energy is spread across the bars, and keeping only the first one is a much bigger intervention. The energy in that first bar as a fraction of the total is the energy-ratio coherence attribute, which is why this filter and that attribute keep appearing together.

5Always look at what you threw away

Input minus output

The filtered section will look better whatever you do to it, because smoothing always makes a picture calmer. The honest check is the difference between input and output: the part of the data the filter decided was noise. If a reflector is visible in that panel, the filter removed signal and the calmer picture is a lie.

Turn the noise down and the window radius up, and watch reflectors appear in the rejected panel. That is the failure mode this display exists to catch. AASPI outputs the rejected noise as a volume for exactly this reason.

6Two small passes, not one big one

Same reach, different weights, less arithmetic

A 7-point filter can be built two ways: apply a 7-point operator once, or apply a 3-point operator three times. They reach equally far. They are not the same filter.

A single pass weights every sample in the window equally, so the sample at the far edge counts as much as the one at the center. Cascading small operators produces weights that taper toward the edges all by themselves, and a tapered filter follows a curving reflector better because it leans on the far samples less.

The panels below are one-dimensional, and in one dimension the two cost about the same: seven multiply-adds for the single pass against nine for the three short ones. The saving arrives in higher dimensions, where the count grows with the volume of the window rather than with its width. Three passes of a 3×3×3 operator cost 81 operations against 343 for a single 7×7×7, which is the form sof3d actually runs.

What the taper buys—

One further point matters only on genuinely noisy data. If the dip estimate was made on the unfiltered volume then it is itself noisy, and a second pass steered by a bad dip field will smear along the wrong direction. AASPI's advice is to send the filtered volume back through dip3d before the next pass, so the dips improve along with the data.

Structure-oriented filtering is almost never the end product. It is the step that runs before everything else, and its effects propagate.

  • Every attribute downstream inherits it. Coherence, curvature and aberrancy are all derivatives of one kind or another, and differentiating amplifies noise. Filtering first is usually the difference between a curvature volume you can interpret and a curvature volume that is mostly speckle. Filtering too hard first is how the small fault you were looking for stops existing.
  • It changes what an autopicker can do. A horizon tracker follows peaks. Random noise creates false peaks for it to jump to, and structure-oriented filtering removes most of them without moving the real ones, because it smooths along the reflector rather than across it.
  • Acquisition footprint is the classic target. The striping that follows the shooting direction is coherent, so it survives filters that only fight random noise, but it cuts across the geology — and a filter aligned with the geology is aligned against the footprint.
  • The rejected volume is the deliverable that keeps you honest. On real data nobody can prove the filter removed only noise. The one defense is to look at what came out, every time, on the sections that matter.
  • It is not a substitute for processing. If the data are noisy because the migration velocity was wrong, a filter aligned along the resulting mispositioned reflectors will happily make the wrong answer look convincing.

Try this

  1. Break the horizontal filter. Why bother: it shows that the shape of a window decides what a filter destroys, before any parameter has been tuned. In step 1, take the fault throw to zero and look at the two panels below the fault, then put it back up to its maximum and look again.
    HintThe horizontal filter loses several times more amplitude on the steeply dipping flank than the dip-steered one does, and the loss grows with dip. The general rule: a horizontal filter of half-width J starts destroying a reflector once the dip moves it more than about a quarter of a wavelength across the window. It is not that dip steering is a refinement — an unsteered filter is simply wrong everywhere the beds are not flat.
  2. Find where alpha-trimmed beats both of its end members. Why bother: the mean and the median are the two filters people actually reach for, and here neither of them wins. Put bad samples at 40% and random noise at 30%, then read the four errors in step 3 at α = 0, 0.25 and 0.5.
    HintThe error dips in the middle: it is lowest at α = 0.25 and higher at both ends, though not by a large margin. Notice first that the two ends are not approximations of anything: the α = 0 figure is identical to the mean and the α = 0.5 figure is identical to the median, because at those settings the alpha-trimmed mean is those filters. The mean is dragged around by every spike; the median ignores the spikes but keeps one value instead of averaging five, so it does less about the random noise. In between, both problems are handled and the measured error is lower than at either end. That is the entire argument for the filter.
  3. Make LUM do nothing. Why bother: leaving clean data alone is a property worth knowing about, because it decides what is safe to run everywhere. Turn both noise sliders to zero and watch the LUM error.
    HintLUM does about half the damage the mean does. On data with nothing wrong with it, LUM only changes a sample that falls outside the range of its neighbors, and hardly any do; the mean smooths everything whether or not it needed smoothing. Now put the noise sliders back up and read step 3 again: LUM is the worst of the four there. That is the trade, and it is the reason the filter exists — LUM is the safest one to run when you are not sure the data need filtering, and the least effective when they do.
  4. Catch a filter stealing signal. Why bother: the rejected part is the only place a filter’s mistakes are visible. The output always looks better. In step 5, set the noise sliders low, the window radius to 5 and the vertical half-window to 7.
    HintReflectors appear in the rejected panel and the signal fraction climbs. The filter has run out of noise to remove and has started on the data. Notice that the filtered panel still looks perfectly reasonable on its own — that is why the rejected panel is not optional.
  5. Watch the PC filter at the fault. Why bother: structure-oriented filtering is sold on preserving faults, and this is where that claim gets tested. Move the analysis point onto the fault plane in step 4 with the throw at maximum.
    HintThe eigenvalue spectrum flattens: the traces on the two sides of the fault are no longer scaled copies of one waveform, so no single eigenvector explains them. The filter reconstructs from the first one anyway, which smears the discontinuity — and this is precisely the failure that the edge preservation in module 02 exists to prevent.

The main points

  • The direction matters more than the filter. Every filter here is respectable. Pointed horizontally at a dipping reflector, all of them destroy it.
  • Dip steering is why this needs dip3d first. Structure-oriented filtering cannot run without a dip volume, which is why it sits where it does in the workflow.
  • Mean and median are the two ends of one filter. The alpha-trimmed mean is both, and everything in between, on one slider.
  • LUM is the conservative choice. It leaves alone anything its neighbors agree with, so it is the least likely to invent a smooth answer where the data do not support one.
  • The principal component filter uses the whole window. More information, a stronger result on coherent data, and a worse one across an edge, where no single waveform describes the traces.
  • Small windows applied several times beat one large window. Tapered weights, better behavior on curved reflectors, and less arithmetic.
  • Look at the rejected noise every time. It is the only display that can tell you the filter went too far.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The model is a layered section with a fold, an optional normal fault, band-limited random noise and isolated bad samples. It is built twice from the same random seed: once clean and once contaminated. Every error figure quoted on the page is the root-mean-square difference between a filtered result and that clean version, measured on the running page rather than estimated.

Dip is estimated from the contaminated section by the semblance dip scan of the first module set, on a decimated grid, and interpolated back to full resolution. Gathering along that dip uses linear interpolation, because a dip of a fraction of a sample per trace almost never lands on a sample.

The filters follow the sof3d documentation directly. The alpha-trimmed mean discards α(J−1) values from each end of the sorted window and averages the rest, so α = 0 gives the mean and α = 0.5 gives the median. LUM replaces the center value only if it falls outside the trimmed range. The principal component filter builds the covariance matrix between traces, takes its first eigenvector, and reconstructs each trace as its projection onto that eigenvector. The Hilbert-transform term in the covariance is included, which is what makes the result insensitive to where the window sits on the wavelet.

Where this departs from AASPI, and why. This is a 2D section, so the window is a line of traces rather than a rectangle or ellipse of them, and there is no crossline dip. The first eigenvector is found by power iteration rather than by a full eigen-decomposition, which is enough when only the dominant one is needed. Three parameters make production structure-oriented filtering usable: the Kuwahara window search and the similarity thresholds slow, shigh and scenter. They are deliberately absent here, so that module 02 can introduce them against the damage they repair.

The cascading demonstration in step 6 uses the mean filter, because the effective-weight argument is exact for a linear filter and only approximate for the others.

Where the meters come from. The model is built in samples and traces; nothing in the filters uses a velocity or a bin size. The scale panel converts, using an interval velocity of 2800 m/s and a trace spacing of 12.5 m, so that a filter window can be quoted as a footprint on the ground. Both numbers are assumed rather than derived, and either can be read as a different survey by scaling everything the panel reports.

The panels are not the same kind of picture. The section is an amplitude, a signed number with a sign convention behind it. The difference panels are the part a filter removed, which is also an amplitude, so those two can be compared directly. The error numbers are not: they are a root-mean-square distance from the clean model, which exists only because this is a synthetic. No such number is available on real data, which is why the visual comparison matters more than the ranking.

What the model leaves out. The noise here is band-limited and laterally correlated, which resembles a migrated section, but the model has no multiples, no acquisition footprint, and no residual migration smile — three of the things structure-oriented filtering is most often deployed against. The fault is a clean rigid offset with no damage zone, no drag and no fault-plane reflection. Real faults come with a few traces of genuinely disturbed data around them, and that zone is harder for every filter here than the sharp discontinuity in this model.

Fault dip, and why the drawn one is steeper. The dip is specified in the ground and converted to a slope on the section through the velocity and the bin spacing, so the plane is at 65° in the rock rather than 65° on the screen. The section is vertically exaggerated, as nearly every seismic display is, and the scale panel reports the drawn dip alongside the true one so the difference can be seen rather than inferred. Fault dips measured off a section are routinely too steep for this reason alone.
