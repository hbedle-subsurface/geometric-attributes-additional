The co-occurrence matrix: throwing the amplitudes away on purpose

Texture attributes begin with a step none of the others take. The amplitudes are discarded and replaced by their rank on a scale of 33 levels. Then even the levels are discarded in favor of a tally: how often does a sample at level i sit next to a sample at level j? That tally is a 33 by 33 table, and every texture attribute in the next module is one number squeezed out of it. This module builds the table.

1From amplitudes to levels

The step that makes it texture rather than amplitude

The window of data is scaled so that its amplitudes land on a small set of integers — 33 of them by default, numbered 0 to 32, with zero amplitude at the middle level. Anything beyond 1.5 standard deviations of the local distribution is pushed onto the end level rather than being allowed to stretch the scale.

After this the actual millivolts are gone. Two windows with identical patterns and very different amplitudes quantize to the same set of levels and produce the same texture attributes. Whether that is a feature or a problem depends on what you are asking, and it is the single most important thing to know about this family of attributes.

2Counting who sits next to whom

Every neighboring pair, tallied into a table

Walk over the quantized window. At every sample, look at its neighbor in a chosen direction, read off the two levels i and j, and add one to the box at row i, column j of a table. Repeat for every sample and every direction. Divide the finished table by the total count so it sums to one, and that table is the gray-level co-occurrence matrix.

The matrix is symmetric, because each pair is counted both ways round. It is also almost entirely empty. A 33-level matrix has 1089 boxes, and a small window has only a few hundred pairs to put in them. Where those pairs land is the whole message.

3Reading a matrix on sight

Diagonal, spread, and clumps

Three patterns cover almost everything you will see, and once you can name them the texture attributes of the next module stop being arbitrary.

  • Everything on the diagonal. Neighbors have the same level as each other. That is smooth, continuous data — a conformable reflector package.
  • Spread away from the diagonal. Neighbors differ a lot. That is rough data: noise, a chaotic package, or a genuine edge.
  • A tight clump somewhere. Only a few levels occur at all, so the window is dominated by one or two amplitudes. A dim, uniform interval does this.

The three windows below are taken from different parts of the same section, and the fourth follows wherever you put the analysis point. The first and third are snapped onto a reflector on purpose. Quantization scales each window by its own standard deviation. So a window sitting in the quiet gap between two reflectors is scaled by its own noise, and reports the texture of that noise rather than of the geology. That is not a quirk of the display — it is how these attributes behave, and it is worth knowing before you interpret a texture volume.

The mean level step is the average distance between a sample's level and its neighbor's, divided by the full scale. It runs from 0 for a matrix entirely on the diagonal to 1 for one entirely in the corners. That is the crudest possible summary the matrix admits, and it already separates the three windows — a fair warning that the eight measures of the next module are not eight independent pieces of information.

4Which neighbor, and how many

Direction, window size, and structural dip

"The neighbor" is not one thing. East is the trace beside you at the same level; north is the sample above; northeast and northwest are the diagonals. A single direction makes the matrix sensitive to features that run one way — which is useful if you know the orientation you care about, and misleading if you do not. AASPI counts all four and adds them, so the answer does not depend on how the survey happens to be oriented.

Window size trades detail for reliability in the usual way, with one extra twist that matters here. A 33 by 33 matrix has 1089 boxes, and a window of 13 traces by 3 samples supplies only a few hundred pairs. Most boxes are empty not because that combination cannot happen but because there were not enough pairs to find out. Attributes computed on a sparse matrix are noisy, and entropy in particular is badly biased by it.

One thing the window does not do is stay level. Like everything else in this set, the window is gathered along structural dip, so that a dipping conformable package quantizes to a set of near-identical rows rather than to a staircase. Without that step the matrix would report structure as texture, which is the same failure disorder has in module 03 when steering is switched off.

5Why the data are clipped first

One bright sample, and 33 levels to spend

Quantization has a fixed budget of levels and has to decide what range to spend them on. If the range is set by the largest sample in the window, then a single bright spike takes the top level and every ordinary sample is squeezed into the few levels near the middle. The matrix collapses onto a small clump, the texture measures all report "uniform", and the window looks featureless because of one sample.

Clipping at a fixed number of standard deviations fixes this. AASPI's default is 1.5σ, which puts about 87% of a Gaussian distribution inside the range and pushes the rest onto the end levels. The outliers are not deleted; they are just prevented from setting the scale.

The bad samples slider adds isolated wild values to the data, which is what gives the clip something to protect against. Turn it to zero and the two matrices below become nearly identical, which is the honest answer to "does the clip matter": only when there is an outlier.

What the clip is buying here—

The co-occurrence matrix is not an attribute you would ever display. It is the intermediate object that decides what the texture attributes can possibly say.

  • Discarding the amplitudes is the point, and the risk. Texture attributes describe pattern independently of brightness, which is what lets them separate two facies of similar amplitude. It also means a bright sand and a dim sand with the same internal pattern are, to these attributes, the same thing. If amplitude is your discriminator, this family will throw it away for you.
  • The matrix is what you are really choosing when you set the parameters. Levels, clip and window size are not cosmetic. They determine how sparse the matrix is, and every attribute in module 05 inherits that sparsity.
  • These attributes were built to be fed to a machine, not read by a person. The AASPI documentation is direct about it: the images are fuzzy and not very useful for human interpretation, and their purpose is to be input to clustering — self-organizing maps, generative topographic mapping, neural networks, random forests. Judging a GLCM volume by whether it looks good on a section is judging it by the wrong criterion.
  • Parameter consistency matters more than parameter optimality. Since the attributes go into a classifier, what breaks the classifier is computing them with different settings on different parts of the survey, not choosing 25 levels rather than 33.
  • The Hilbert transform is in there for a reason. AASPI computes the matrix on the data and on its Hilbert transform and adds them. That removes the dependence on where the analysis window happens to sit on the wavelet — the same trick, and the same reason, as in the principal component filter of module 01.

Try this

  1. Make the matrix collapse onto its diagonal. Set the noise and the chaotic package to zero, then put the analysis point in the conformable section above the chaotic package.
    HintNearly all the weight lands on or beside the diagonal, because neighbors along a dipping smooth reflector have almost the same level. That is what a texture attribute means by "homogeneous", and it is why homogeneity and coherence produce such similar-looking maps despite being computed in completely different ways.
  2. Starve the matrix. In step 4, set the gray levels to 49 and the window radius to 2.
    HintAround one box in fifty is occupied. There are 2401 of them at 49 levels, and a five-trace window three samples tall supplies only 76 pairs to fill them with. Any attribute computed from that matrix is mostly reporting which boxes happened to get a pair, which changes from sample to sample for no geological reason. Drop the levels to 5 with the same window and the occupancy rises severalfold; widen the window back to 12 traces at 49 levels and it rises again, though not as far. Fewer levels on a small window is not a compromise, it is the correct choice, and the two parameters have to be set together.
  3. See what a single direction misses. In step 4, switch to "north only" and put the analysis point on a steeply dipping flank.
    HintThe vertical direction crosses the wavelet, so it reports large level differences even on a perfectly clean reflector, and the matrix spreads. East, along the reflector, does not. A single-direction matrix measures the feature and its orientation mixed together, which is exactly what counting all four directions and adding avoids.
  4. Let one spike set the scale. In step 5, compare the two matrices with the noise slider around 40%.
    HintAt the settings the module opens with, the largest sample in the window sits several standard deviations out, and the bulk of the window spreads over roughly twice as many levels with the clip as without it — half the detail, thrown away to make room for one sample. Set the bad samples slider to zero and the difference disappears, because there is no longer an outlier to protect against. That is the whole argument, and it is why the parameter is there at all.
  5. Find the setting where clipping stops helping. In step 5, drag the clip slider from 0.5σ up to 4σ and watch the levels-used curve.
    HintVery tight clipping pushes most of the window onto the two end levels, which destroys the detail you were trying to measure. Very loose clipping is the same as not clipping at all. The curve has a broad flat top, and 1.5σ sits on it — which is a more honest reason for the default than any derivation would be.

The main points

  • Quantization throws the amplitudes away. What survives is rank within the local window, on a scale of 33 levels by default.
  • The clip stops one sample from setting the scale. 1.5σ is the AASPI default and it keeps most of a Gaussian inside the range.
  • The matrix counts neighboring pairs, not samples. Box (i, j) holds how often a sample at level i sat beside one at level j.
  • Four directions are counted and added. That makes the result independent of how the feature is oriented.
  • On the diagonal means smooth; spread means rough; a clump means uniform. Those three readings cover most of what the matrix ever says.
  • The matrix is nearly always sparse. A thousand boxes and a few hundred pairs, which is why levels and window size have to be chosen together.
  • Nothing here is displayed. The matrix exists to be reduced to the numbers of module 05.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The model is the section used in module 03: conformable packages above and below a middle package that can be made chaotic, with an optional normal fault and band-limited random noise. Dip comes from the semblance scan of the first module set, and the analysis window is gathered along it with linear interpolation.

Quantization follows the AASPI description. The standard deviation of the window is computed, and the range is set to plus and minus the clip factor times that standard deviation. The amplitudes are then mapped linearly onto the integers 0 to L−1, with zero amplitude at the middle level. Anything outside the range is assigned the end level rather than discarded. The matrix counts every neighboring pair in the chosen directions, adds each pair in both orientations so that the result is symmetric, and divides by the total count.

Where this departs from AASPI. Two dimensions rather than three, so the four directions are the four in a section rather than the larger set available in a 3D window, and there is no crossline window radius. AASPI computes the matrix on the data and on its Hilbert transform and adds the two, weighting each one-sample-thick slice within the analysis window by its RMS amplitude. The module computes the data matrix alone, which keeps the displays legible. Module 05 shows what the Hilbert term changes. The vertical window here is three samples, matching the AASPI default of one sample interval either side.

Every number quoted on this page is measured from the running page. The matrices at 33 levels are computed at the analysis point only; the whole-section maps in module 05 use fewer levels for speed, and that difference is stated there.
