Eight measures, and how many of them are really different

Haralick proposed fourteen numbers to squeeze out of a co-occurrence matrix and AASPI computes eight of them. They have distinct names and distinct formulas, and a good part of the work of using them is finding out which ones are telling you the same thing twice. This module computes them all on the same data and lets you check.

1Contrast, dissimilarity, homogeneity

The same question asked three ways

These three all ask how far the matrix sits from its diagonal. They differ only in how much they care about the boxes that are a long way out:

contrast      C = Σ Pij (i−j)2
dissimilarity  D = Σ Pij |i−j|
homogeneity   H = Σ Pij / (1 + (i−j)2)

Contrast squares the distance, so a single pair of very different neighbors dominates it. Dissimilarity uses the distance itself, so it is far less swayed by one outlier. Homogeneity uses the reciprocal, so it is largest when everything is near the diagonal and it barely notices how far out the far pairs are. Three answers to "how rough", weighted for three different tolerances of outliers.

2Energy and entropy

Not how strong, but how predictable

The next two ignore where the weight sits and ask only how it is spread out.

energy  E = ( Σ Pij2 )1/2
entropy S = − Σ Pij ln Pij

Energy here has nothing to do with amplitude. The name is inherited from signal processing and it is actively misleading on seismic data. It is large when the weight is concentrated in a few boxes, which happens when the window is monotonous. The AASPI documentation makes the point sharply: if every voxel in the window is the same, even if that value is zero, the energy is high. A dead trace scores maximum energy.

Entropy is its opposite in behavior, large when the weight is spread evenly over many boxes. The two are so closely anti-correlated that computing both is nearly always redundant. Entropy is the one AASPI recommends, alongside homogeneity.

3Mean, variance and correlation

Where two of them turn out to be old friends

The last three treat the matrix as a probability distribution and take its ordinary statistics.

mean     μ = Σ j Pij
variance V = Σ Pij (i − μ)2
correlation R = (1/V) Σ Pij (i − μ)(j − μ)

Mean is the average gray level in the window. The levels came from quantizing the window against its own standard deviation, so this is close to a constant everywhere. AASPI says plainly that it has little value in either interpretation or machine learning.

Variance is the spread of levels, and the AASPI documentation states the consequence directly: it reproduces semblance-based similarity, up to a scaling and a change of sign, and adds almost nothing to it. The panel below checks that claim against the semblance coherence of the first module set rather than asking you to take it on trust. The coherence is computed with the same lateral window as the texture measures, so that the comparison is about the two attributes and not about two different window sizes.

Correlation is the one whose everyday meaning does not transfer. On a photograph it measures whether neighboring pixels resemble each other. On seismic data it does the opposite of what you would expect, as the documentation notes. Where amplitudes in the window are all close to the mean the correlation is low. Where they differ it is high.

6On a horizon slice

Where these attributes are actually read

Everything so far has been on a vertical section, which is the honest place to learn the arithmetic and the wrong place to use it. A texture attribute computed on a section is largely measuring the pattern the wavelet makes. The strongest thing in any vertical window is the peak-trough-peak of the wavelet itself, which is why the Hilbert term of step 2 has to exist.

Sliced along a horizon, the same attribute is measuring the pattern the geology makes. Amplitude changes laterally because the rock changes laterally, and the texture of that change is the facies. This is how a GLCM volume normally gets looked at, and it is why the AASPI examples are horizon and stratal slices rather than lines.

The map below is an amplitude extraction with four things on it: conformable background, a chaotic body, a channel with bars across its axis, and a fault lineament. All four are around the same brightness on purpose. An amplitude map struggles to separate them; the texture measures do not, because they are not looking at brightness.

In map view the four directions are the four compass directions, which makes the anisotropy visible rather than theoretical. The channel is banded across its axis and smooth along it, so a north-only matrix and an east-only matrix disagree about it by a large factor while they agree almost exactly on the background. Summing all four directions, which is what AASPI does, buys an answer that does not depend on how the channel happens to be oriented. It also gives up the ability to tell you that it is oriented at all.

Texture attributes are not read; they are fed to something. That changes what makes a good choice among them.

  • Choose for independence, not for beauty. The attribute that looks best on a section is usually the one most like coherence, which you already have. What a classifier needs is measurements that disagree — and step 5 is how you find out which those are on your data rather than on somebody else's.
  • Entropy and homogeneity are the recommended default. That is the AASPI documentation's own advice, and the correlation table usually supports it: they sit on opposite sides of the redundancy structure.
  • Do not read GLCM energy as amplitude. It is the single most misleading name in the whole attribute vocabulary. A window of constant zeros has maximum GLCM energy.
  • GLCM variance duplicates semblance. If you already compute coherence, variance is a second copy of it with different units. Spend the slot on something else.
  • These attributes reward a stable acquisition and processing history. Because the matrix is built from local rank rather than amplitude, changes in the processing that alter the noise character between survey vintages show up as texture changes that look exactly like facies changes.
  • The classifier inherits every parameter choice. Levels, clip, window size and whether the Hilbert term was included all propagate into the clusters. Two volumes computed with different settings cannot be compared, and the settings are rarely recorded in the map that gets presented.

Try this

  1. Separate contrast from dissimilarity. In step 1, put the analysis point in the middle of the chaotic package and read the share of contrast carried by the ten most distant pairs.
    HintA small number of extreme pairs carries a large share of the contrast, because contrast squares the distance. Dissimilarity weights the same pairs linearly and so reports something much closer to the typical behavior of the window. When the two attributes disagree on your data, that is what the disagreement means: contrast has found outliers, dissimilarity has found the bulk.
  2. Confirm the energy trap. Set the noise and the chaotic package to zero and put the analysis point in a quiet interval between two reflectors.
    HintThe energy is high — the window is monotonous, so the matrix has almost all of its weight in one box, and that is what energy measures. There is no seismic energy there at all. If you handed this map to somebody and called it "energy" they would read it as an amplitude map and be wrong everywhere it matters.
  3. Check the variance claim yourself. In step 3, read the correlation between GLCM variance and semblance coherence, then change the window radius and read it again.
    HintAt the settings the module opens with it reads strongly negative, and it stays strongly negative whatever you do, because the two are measuring the same thing with opposite signs. The AASPI documentation says variance provides almost no additional interpretational value; this is the arithmetic behind that sentence, and you can watch it hold as the parameters change rather than taking it on faith. Notice also that narrowing the window pulls the correlation toward zero — not because the two attributes stop agreeing, but because a smaller window makes both of them noisier.
  4. Find a genuinely independent pair. In step 5, look for the palest box in the correlation table.
    HintAt the opening settings the palest boxes involve mean — it correlates with variance and with entropy at a few hundredths, which is to say it is independent of the others because it is barely responding to anything at all. That is a warning about reading the table too quickly: an attribute can be independent because it carries different information or because it carries none, and only looking at its map tells you which. Contrast and dissimilarity, at better than 0.95, are the clearest redundancy; energy and entropy, nearly as strongly and with the opposite sign, are the next. Three components account for 90% of the variance in all eight, and the first alone takes 64%.
  5. Watch a channel become invisible. In step 6, switch the mapped attribute to mean, then to homogeneity.
    HintGLCM mean reads within a couple of percent of the background on the channel, a difference no color bar will render visible. Homogeneity separates the two by better than a tenth and the channel is obvious. The reason mean fails is that every window was quantized against its own standard deviation, so the channel being brighter was normalized away before the matrix was ever built; what survives is that the channel has bars across it and the background does not. That is the case for texture attributes in one pair of images — the feature is a pattern, not an amplitude, and no amount of tuning turns an amplitude map into something that finds it.
  6. Turn the Hilbert term off. Uncheck it in the header and watch step 4.
    HintThe maps become banded: the attribute changes with the phase of the wavelet, so it oscillates vertically at the frequency of the data. Adding the matrix of the Hilbert transform removes that, because where the data are at a peak the Hilbert transform is at a zero crossing and vice versa. It is the same device, for the same reason, as the Hilbert term in the principal component filter of module 01.

The main points

  • Contrast, dissimilarity and homogeneity are one question with three weights. Squared distance, distance, and reciprocal distance from the diagonal.
  • GLCM energy is not amplitude. It measures concentration in the matrix, and a window of constant values scores maximum.
  • Entropy is energy's opposite and the more useful of the two. AASPI recommends entropy and homogeneity as the default pair.
  • GLCM mean says almost nothing, because quantization already normalized each window.
  • GLCM variance is semblance in different units. The documentation says so and the crossplot in step 3 confirms it.
  • GLCM correlation does not mean what it means on a photograph. A uniform window scores low, and so does a window of random values; what scores high is a window whose levels change smoothly across it, which on seismic data means a wavelet passing through.
  • Eight measures are not eight facts. On this section three components carry 90% of the variance in all eight. Check the redundancy on your own data before choosing what to feed a classifier.
  • The Hilbert term removes the dependence on wavelet phase. Without it the attributes band vertically at the frequency of the data.
  • On a section these measure the wavelet; on a horizon slice they measure the geology. The second is what they are for, and it is how AASPI displays them.
  • In map view the directions are compass directions. A feature with a grain reads differently east and north, and summing all four trades that information for an answer that does not depend on orientation.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The co-occurrence matrices are built exactly as in module 04. The window is gathered along structural dip and quantized against its own standard deviation with a 1.5σ clip, and all four directions are counted with each pair added both ways round. The eight measures are then computed from those matrices with the formulas printed in steps 1 to 3, which are the forms given in the glcm3d documentation.

The Hilbert term. When the checkbox is on, a second matrix is built from the Hilbert transform of the same window and the two are added inside each measure, which is what AASPI does. It removes the dependence on where the analysis window happens to sit on the wavelet. AASPI additionally weights the contribution of each one-sample-thick slice by its RMS amplitude; that weighting is not applied here, and the difference it makes is small on a section with no strong amplitude gradient.

Where this departs from AASPI. Two dimensions rather than three. The maps use fewer gray levels than the 33-level default, and the slider tops out at 17. A map requires a matrix at every sample, and a sparse matrix produces a noisy map. The single-point matrices in module 04 use the full 33. That is a real effect and not only a speed compromise: it is the same sparsity argument module 04 makes, seen from the other side. AASPI computes fifteen measures in total, including Gao's randomness; the eight here are the ones the GUI offers as output volumes.

The map view in step 6 is a synthetic amplitude extraction rather than a slice through a computed volume. It is ng by ng bins carrying four facies, each given a texture rather than only a brightness, since a texture attribute cannot see brightness. The background varies slowly, the chaotic body is fine random speckle, the channel is banded across its axis and smooth along it, and the fault is a narrow dim lineament. The measures are computed on it exactly as they are on the section, with a square window and the same four directions. In map view those are the four compass directions, the sense Haralick originally defined them in. The window there is capped at 8 bins radius and the maps are computed every other bin, for speed.

The redundancy analysis in step 5 standardizes each measure to zero mean and unit variance before computing the correlation matrix and its eigenvalues. The eight have wildly different units, and an unstandardized analysis would simply rank them by magnitude.
