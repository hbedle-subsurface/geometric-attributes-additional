Nonparallelism: are the reflectors parallel to each other?

Coherence asks whether neighboring traces look alike. Disorder asks whether the data are rough. Neither asks the question a stratigrapher asks first, which is whether the reflectors in a package run parallel to one another or wander independently. Salt, mass transport complexes and karst collapse all fail that test while often passing the other two, and this is the attribute that catches them.

1Every dip is an arrow

And parallel means the arrows agree

Take the inline and crossline dip components at a point, p and q, and turn them into a unit vector pointing perpendicular to the reflector:

n = (p, q, 1) / (p2 + q2 + 1)1/2

A package of parallel reflectors gives a tight cluster of these arrows all pointing the same way. A chaotic package gives arrows pointing everywhere. That is the entire idea, and everything that follows is a way of putting a number on how tight the cluster is.

Note what the measurement does not care about: which way the arrows point. A steeply dipping but perfectly conformable package has arrows that all lean together, and its deviation is as low as a flat one's. This is a measurement of agreement, not of dip.

2Deviation of vector dip

The scatter, mapped everywhere

Averaging the arrows in the window gives a mean direction; measuring how far the individual arrows sit from that mean gives the attribute:

μk = Σ ej nkj / Σ ej
σn = { Σ ej Σk (nkj − μk)2 / Σ ej }1/2

The weights ej are the energy, or RMS amplitude, at each point. That weighting matters more than it looks. A dip estimate made where the reflection is weak is mostly measuring noise. Without the weights, those bad estimates would count as heavily as the good ones. Switch the weighting off in the header and watch the map get noisier where the amplitudes are low.

3Deviation of energy gradient

The same statistic, applied to amplitude

The second attribute changes what is measured and keeps the arithmetic. Instead of the dip vectors it takes the lateral gradient of energy: how fast reflection strength is changing, and in which direction. That gradient is rotated into the plane of the reflector. The attribute is then how much it varies across the window.

The rotation matters. An energy gradient measured on a steeply dipping reflector is partly an artifact of the dip: you are sampling the reflector obliquely. Dividing by (1 + p2)1/2 removes that, so what is left is amplitude variation along the bed rather than variation caused by geometry.

This one lights up boundaries rather than interiors. The edge of a salt body is where reflection strength changes fastest, so that is where the gradient deviation peaks. The middle of the body, where the amplitudes are uniformly poor, can be quiet.

4Putting the two together

A 2×2 matrix and its larger eigenvalue

The third attribute asks whether the two deviations are large in the same places. The two are normalized onto a common footing and arranged as a 2×2 covariance matrix. The attribute is its larger eigenvalue. That is the same device as the energy-ratio coherence of the first module set, applied to a much smaller matrix.

The word covariance is doing less work here than it usually does. The diagonal entries are mean squared deviations, so they cannot be negative, and the off-diagonal entry is built from the square root of the product of the two magnitudes rather than from a signed product of centered variables. It therefore has no sign to report: it says whether the two deviations are large at the same samples, not whether they rise and fall together. Two places where a reader will notice the difference: the matrix cannot report anti-correlation, and the larger eigenvalue is never smaller than the larger diagonal entry, so the attribute cannot come out quieter than whichever of its two inputs is louder. The correlation between the two deviations reported below is an ordinary signed correlation computed over the whole map, and it is not the off-diagonal entry.

Because an eigenvalue responds to either input, this attribute lights up where dip is chaotic, where energy is changing fast, and most strongly where both happen at once. The AASPI documentation puts it plainly: the covariance expresses both the chaotic and the high-energy features, showing the merged anomalies of the first two.

That merging is a convenience and a hazard. One map is easier to look at than two, and a bright anomaly on it no longer tells you which of the two measurements produced it. The two-panel crossplot below is the honest version: where a point sits in it says which attribute is responding.

A low correlation between the two deviations means they are finding different things, which is the case for combining them. A high one means the covariance is a more expensive way of computing something you already have.

5Four features, three attributes

Which one to reach for

The model contains a chaotic body, a fault, a dimmed channel with no structural expression at all, and conformable background. Each attribute has a different opinion about which of those is interesting, and the table below is the whole argument for computing more than one.

The channel is the interesting row. It is a dimming with no structural expression whatsoever: the reflector runs straight through it. So the dip deviation has nothing to report, and only the attributes that look at amplitude find it. If you were mapping channels with dip deviation alone you would miss it entirely, and nothing in the map would tell you that you had.

Response is the mean attribute value over a feature minus the background mean, divided by the spread of the attribute across the map. Scaling it that way is what lets three attributes with different units be ranked against each other. Above about 0.3 the feature is visible on the map; below about 0.1 the attribute cannot see it at all. Compare down a column to ask what one attribute finds; compare across a row to choose which attribute to compute.

Nonparallelism exists because the seismic facies that matter most in exploration are often the ones that coherence handles worst.

  • Salt, mass transport complexes and karst collapse are defined by non-parallelism. Not by being incoherent — the interior of a mass transport complex can be locally quite coherent — but by reflectors that do not run with each other. This attribute measures that property directly instead of hoping a discontinuity detector will notice.
  • It is built for machine learning, and it shows. The documentation frames the whole family as an answer to "which dip is useful for machine learning in differentiating seismic facies", and the answer it gives is a statistic of dip rather than dip itself. These are volumes to cluster, not volumes to contour.
  • Energy weighting is not a detail. Dip estimated where there is no reflection is noise, and a statistic that counts it equally is a statistic of the noise. The weighting is what makes the attribute usable on real data with real amplitude variation.
  • The salt-and-pepper problem is real and documented. The deviation of vector dip and the covariance both highlight salt domes and both produce scattered spurious anomalies. Expect to smooth the result, and expect that smoothing to cost you the small features.
  • The two deviations agreeing is a fact about your geology, not about the attributes. The documentation makes this point about the Great South Basin example: dip and amplitude change in the same places there because faults and progradation change both. On different geology they separate.
  • Window size is inherited from dip3d. AASPI defaults the analysis window to double the one used to compute the dips, which is a reminder that this attribute is a statistic of an estimate — everything wrong with the dip volume arrives here first.

Try this

  1. Confirm it ignores dip itself. Why bother: the attribute rests on separating how steep the dip is from how much the dip varies, and this is that separation. In step 1, move the analysis point from the flat part of the map onto the flank of the dome and watch σdip.
    HintOn the flat background the dip and σdip are both a couple of degrees, which is where the module opens. On the flank of the dome the dip climbs several times over and σdip hardly moves, because the arrows on the flank all lean the same way as each other. Move into the chaotic body and the dip is similar again while σdip jumps past 8°. Steep and parallel reads low; gently dipping and disordered reads high. Anyone who reads this attribute as a dip magnitude map will get it exactly backwards on a monocline.
  2. Turn the energy weighting off. Why bother: the weighting is on by default and changes what the attribute reports over dim areas, which is where mistakes get made. Uncheck it in the header, then look at step 2 over the channel.
    HintThe dimmed channel starts producing dip-deviation anomalies it should not have, because the dips picked on a weak reflection are unreliable and, unweighted, they count as much as the good ones. The weighting is what stops the attribute from mapping the quality of its own input.
  3. Find the feature dip deviation cannot see. Why bother: knowing where an attribute is blind is worth more than knowing where it is strong. In step 5, read the channel row.
    HintAt the opening settings dip deviation barely registers on the channel while the gradient deviation reads several times as much — not a large number in absolute terms, but a clear separation, and the channel is visible on one map and not on the other. The reason is that the channel is a pure amplitude feature: the reflector runs straight through it without bending, so there is nothing there for a statistic of dip to find. This is the clearest case in the module for computing both. Neither attribute is more sensitive than the other; they are sensitive to different things, and no amount of tuning turns one into the other.
  4. Make the covariance redundant. Why bother: two attributes that agree everywhere are one attribute wearing two names. In step 4, turn the channel dimming to zero.
    HintWith the only pure-amplitude feature gone, the two deviations start agreeing, because everything that remains changes dip and amplitude together. The covariance is then a more expensive way of computing what either one already told you. Put the channel back and they separate again. That is what the correlation figure in that step is for — it tells you whether the third attribute is earning its place on your data.
  5. Buy resolution and sell it back. Why bother: window size trades detail against stability, and here the trade can be read in meters. Set the window radius to 1, then to 7, watching step 2.
    HintAt radius 1 the map is speckled — a handful of dip estimates is not enough for a standard deviation to mean much, and this is the salt-and-pepper pattern the AASPI documentation warns about. At radius 7 the speckle is gone and so is the edge of the chaotic body, smeared several bins wide. Nothing in the middle of that range is correct; there is a size of feature you are looking for, and the window has to be chosen for it.

The main points

  • Each dip becomes a unit vector normal to the reflector. Parallel reflectors give a tight cluster of those vectors.
  • The attribute is the scatter of the cluster, not its direction. Steep conformable dip reads as low as flat conformable dip.
  • σdip = arcsin σn turns that scatter into degrees, which is something you can talk about.
  • The dips are weighted by energy. A dip measured where there is no reflection is a noise measurement and should not count equally.
  • The energy gradient version finds edges rather than interiors. Salt boundaries, channel margins, amplitude fronts.
  • The covariance is the larger eigenvalue of a 2×2 matrix built from the two, and responds to either — convenient, and no longer diagnostic of which.
  • A pure amplitude feature is invisible to dip deviation. The channel in this model proves it, and nothing on the map would warn you.
  • Expect salt-and-pepper. It is documented behavior, not a bug in your parameters.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The model is a picked horizon in map view. It carries four features on a regional dip: a dome with a gentle roll, a near-straight normal fault, a circular chaotic body whose relief is scrambled by three superposed patterns, and a sinuous channel. The channel dims the reflection strength without touching the structure. Every point carries a label saying which of those it belongs to, which is what lets the response figures on the page be measured rather than judged by eye.

Dip components p and q are central differences of the surface in meters per meter, and the energy is the square of the reflection strength with its own central-difference gradients. The three attributes are computed exactly as printed on the page: the energy-weighted scatter of the unit normals; the scatter of the dip-rotated energy gradient; and the larger eigenvalue of the 2×2 covariance matrix of the two. Before that third one combines them, each is normalized by its own median, so that neither dominates by virtue of its units.

Where this departs from AASPI. The attribute is computed on one picked surface rather than volumetrically, so there is no vertical component to the analysis window and no vertical dip variation. AASPI computes it in a 3D window along structural dip, and its analysis window defaults to double the one used in dip3d. The dip components here come from differentiating a known surface rather than from a semblance scan, which makes them cleaner than real ones. The noise slider adds scatter back to compensate. What that scatter does to the speckliness of the result is the honest part of the trade. The normalization constants inside the covariance are medians of the two deviations over the map. AASPI's are set differently. The choice affects the balance between the two contributions but not the character of the result. The off-diagonal entry of the 2×2 is the mean of the square root of the product of the two normalized deviations, which is the construction step 4 describes: it cannot go negative and it carries no sign. Absolute values from this page are therefore not comparable against a production nonparallelism volume without checking that construction against the program documentation first.

Where the meters come from. This model is built on 25 m bins from the start, so the bin spacing is part of the geometry rather than a display convention, and window radii convert to distance without any further assumption. There is no velocity here because there is no time axis: a horizon slice has already had its time dimension collapsed.

The panels are not the same kind of picture. The map shows amplitude extracted along a pick. Deviation of vector dip is an angle-like spread with the units of dip. The covariance term is a product of two deviations and has neither the units of the first nor a sense in which large is simply worse. Colors that look alike across the panels are not measuring alike, and the crossplot is included because it is the display in which that is hardest to forget.

What the model leaves out. The horizon is a picked surface with no pick errors in it beyond the noise slider, and pick error is the dominant source of apparent nonparallelism on real horizon slices. Nothing here reproduces an autotracker jumping a cycle, which puts a step into the surface that these attributes will report as a genuine structural feature. The salt body has a clean edge, and real salt flanks are imaged badly enough that the attribute is often reading the failure of the migration rather than the geometry of the flank.
