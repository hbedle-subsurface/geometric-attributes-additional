Edge preservation: the fault is signal, not noise

Every filter in module 01 smooths across a fault as happily as it smooths along a reflector, because nothing has told it the difference. Two ideas fix that, and they work in completely different ways. One changes where the window sits. The other decides whether to filter at all. AASPI applies both, and this module takes them apart.

1What a plain filter does to a fault

The problem, before either fix

Put a window across a fault and half of its samples come from the upthrown side and half from the downthrown side. Averaging them produces a value belonging to neither, and doing that at every point along the fault turns a sharp break into a ramp. The fault does not disappear; it goes soft, which is worse, because a soft fault still looks like a fault and no longer tells you where it is.

Coherence run on the filtered volume is the honest test. If the filter has smeared the fault, the coherence anomaly gets broader and weaker — and coherence is usually the reason the data were filtered in the first place.

Both numbers are read off one horizontal line of coherence through the middle of the section. The minimum says how deep the fault anomaly is; the steepness says how sharply coherence falls into it. Smearing shows up as both a shallower minimum and a softer flank.

2Move the window off center

The Kuwahara idea

Nothing says the window has to be centered on the point being filtered. It only has to contain it. So build several candidate windows of the same size, all containing the analysis point but sitting at different offsets, and score each one. The window straddling the fault will have a wide spread of values and a low coherence; the window sitting entirely on one side will not. Choose the best-scoring window and filter with that.

The result is a value taken from one side of the fault rather than an average across it. Kuwahara et al. (1976) did this on medical images; Luo et al. (2002) brought it to seismic data, scoring by smallest standard deviation, and Marfurt (2006) made it structure-oriented and scored by highest coherence instead.

3Or simply refuse to filter

The Fehmers and Höecker weighting

The second idea does not move the window at all. It looks up the coherence at the analysis point and uses it to decide how much of the filtered value to accept:

  • Coherence below slow: an edge. Weight zero — keep the original sample untouched.
  • Coherence above shigh: ordinary data. Weight one — take the filtered value.
  • In between: a linear blend, so the filter fades in rather than switching on.

Everything about this rests on the coherence volume being right. If coherence marks a noise burst as an edge, the filter will politely leave that noise burst alone; if it misses a subtle fault, the filter will smooth it away. The weighting does not detect edges. It only acts on a detection somebody else made.

4Where the thresholds come from

A histogram, and a decision somebody has to make

Most interpreters are surprised to learn how many processing parameters are chosen by eye rather than derived. These three are among them. The AASPI documentation describes the procedure honestly: display coherence with a color bar, raise the lower limit until the real geologic edges are the only things left at the bottom of the scale, raise the upper limit until the acquisition footprint stops being visible, and read the two numbers off the color bar.

The histogram below is the same decision in one picture. The bulk of any coherence volume is a tall peak near one — that is the ordinary, conformable data, which is most of the section. The edges live in the thin tail on the left. slow and shigh go in the gap between the tail and the peak, and the honest reason for choosing a particular pair of values is that the pictures on either side of them look right.

scenter is a different kind of parameter. It is not about edges at all — it is a brake on the Kuwahara search. Where coherence is already high there is no edge to avoid, so letting the window wander off center serves no purpose and does real harm: the filter starts choosing a slightly different offset at every point, and the image acquires a blocky, patchy look in exactly the places you expected it to be smooth. Above scenter, AASPI stops searching and uses the centered window.

5Four ways of running the same filter

Nothing, one, the other, and both

Same mean filter, same window size, same data. The only thing changing is which edge preservation is switched on. Each row carries two numbers: the error against the clean model over the whole section, and the lowest coherence left on the fault. Those two numbers usually disagree about which filter is best, and the disagreement is the point.

Overall error is dominated by the 99% of the section that is ordinary data, so it rewards whichever filter smooths hardest — which is the one with no edge preservation at all. The fault coherence is measured on the one percent you were actually trying to protect. Deciding which number matters is an interpretation decision, not a processing one.

The two things being scored pull against each other, which is the point of the step. Suppressing noise everywhere and keeping the fault sharp are different jobs, and a filter that does one perfectly usually does the other badly. Watch both numbers as you change the window radius.

Edge preservation is the reason structure-oriented filtering is usable at all. Without it, the step that was supposed to make faults easier to see makes them harder.

  • The circularity is real, and worth naming. The filter needs coherence to find the edges; coherence is computed better on filtered data. AASPI's answer is to iterate — filter, recompute dip and coherence on the result, filter again — rather than pretend the problem is not there.
  • The dip volume has to be cleaned up first. A dip scan run across a fault reports a very steep apparent dip, because that is what the data look like there. A Kuwahara window steered by that bad dip reads from the wrong place, and the search then makes the result worse instead of better. AASPI has a separate program, filter_dip_components, for this, and this module runs the short version of it before doing anything else. Skipping it is a quiet way to make edge preservation look useless.
  • A bad coherence volume is worse than none. The weighting obeys whatever it is given. If the coherence volume marks acquisition footprint as edges, the filter carefully preserves the footprint.
  • Thresholds are data-specific and survey-specific. The values that worked on last year's survey are a starting guess and nothing more. Any published triple, including the one this module opens with, is somebody else's picture.
  • Kuwahara filtering sharpens as well as smooths. That is why it is capable of making an image look processed. If the result is blocky where the geology is not, scenter is the parameter to reach for.
  • The stratigraphic edges matter as much as the faults. Channel margins, fan edges and unconformities are all discontinuities, and everything said here about the fault applies to them — with the difference that they are subtler, so they are the first thing an over-aggressive filter removes.

Try this

  1. Measure the smearing. In step 1, set the window radius to 6 and read the two fault-width numbers.
    HintAt the settings the module opens with, the coherence minimum on the fault goes from 0.556 to 0.942 and the flank steepness from 0.180 to 0.021 per trace. Read that again: the plain filter has put back nearly nine tenths of the drop that told you a fault was there. Widening the window makes it worse. Both numbers move the same way — the anomaly ends up shallower and softer, so after filtering you would place the fault less precisely and believe in it less. Now go to step 5 and switch the preservation on at the same radius.
  2. Watch the window jump. In step 2, drag the analysis point slowly across the fault and watch the winning offset.
    HintApproaching from the left, the winner stays centered until the window starts to overlap the fault, then jumps to the offset that keeps it entirely on the left side. It holds there across the fault, then jumps to the right-hand offset. That switch is exactly what preserves the edge — and it is also why the method can make a smooth image look segmented, because the same jump happens on much smaller provocation when there is no real edge.
  3. Put the thresholds in the wrong place. In step 4, set slow and shigh both above the main peak of the histogram.
    HintAlmost everything falls below slow, so almost nothing gets filtered and the noise stays. Then drag both down below the tail: everything is above shigh, the weighting has no effect, and you are back to the plain filter of module 01. The thresholds only do anything when they sit in the gap between the tail and the peak, which is why the histogram is the display worth looking at.
  4. Find out which method does more. In step 5, compare the four error numbers, then raise the noise to 60% and compare again.
    HintAt the opening settings the fault coherence reads 0.942 with no preservation, 0.929 with Kuwahara, 0.928 with the weighting and 0.910 with both — so both together preserve the most, and neither alone gets there. The two work differently, which is why they add: the weighting protects the fault by declining to filter it, so the noise on the fault survives too; the Kuwahara search protects it while still filtering, by reading from one side. Raise the noise and the ranking on overall error will invert, because at that point the noise you left on the fault costs more than the fault you saved.
  5. Take the fault away. Set the throw to zero and look at step 5 again.
    HintThe errors read 0.0117, 0.0132, 0.0123 and 0.0134 — the plain filter is now the best of the four, by about 15%. With no edge to preserve, edge preservation can only cost you: the other three are declining to filter, or filtering off-center, in places where there was nothing to protect. That is the honest limit of the technique. It is insurance, the premium is real, and you buy it because you cannot know in advance where the edges are — which is why you were computing coherence in the first place.

The main points

  • A filter cannot tell a fault from noise. Both are places where neighboring traces disagree. Something outside the filter has to make the distinction.
  • Kuwahara changes where the window reads from. Several candidate windows, all containing the analysis point, only one of them centered; the least disturbed one wins.
  • The coherence weighting changes whether the result is used. Below slow, keep the original; above shigh, take the filtered value; in between, blend.
  • They are independent, and AASPI runs both. One picks the data, the other picks the output.
  • scenter is not a third edge threshold. It switches the Kuwahara search off where the data are already coherent, which is what keeps the result from looking blocky.
  • The thresholds are chosen by looking, not by deriving. The histogram of the coherence volume is where to look.
  • Edge preservation costs something on data with no edges. It is worth paying because you usually cannot know in advance where the edges are — which is, after all, why you are filtering.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The model is the section of module 01 with a normal fault and band-limited random noise, built twice from the same seed so that a clean version exists to measure against. Dip comes from the semblance scan and is then median-filtered on its own grid, which is the short version of AASPI's filter_dip_components; without that step the dip estimate at the fault is contaminated by the fault itself and the Kuwahara search reads from the wrong place. Coherence is the energy-ratio estimate of the first module set, computed along the filtered dip on the unfiltered section.

The Kuwahara search. Candidate windows are the 2J+1 lateral offsets that still contain the analysis point, following the AASPI description for the mean, alpha-trimmed and LUM filters, which act on the dipping plane and therefore only need lateral shifts. Each candidate is scored by the semblance of the data it contains and the highest-scoring one wins, which is Marfurt's (2006) variant; Luo et al.'s original scores by smallest standard deviation instead, and picks the same window nearly all the time. Where the coherence at the analysis point exceeds scenter, the search is skipped and the centered window used, exactly as the documentation specifies.

The weighting. w = 0 below slow, w = 1 above shigh, and w = (s − slow)/(shigh − slow) between them, with the output d = w·dfilt + (1−w)·dorig. This is equation-for-equation what Fehmers and Höecker (2003) describe and what sof3d implements.

Where this departs from AASPI. Two dimensions rather than three, so windows shift laterally along one axis only and the vertical Kuwahara search available to the principal component filter has no analogue here. The filter itself is the mean throughout, so that the panels differ only in their edge preservation; AASPI applies the same machinery to the alpha-trimmed, LUM and principal component filters. The coherence volume is computed once on the unfiltered data rather than iterated, which is the simplification the "Why it matters" tab argues is the most consequential one.

The fault-width statistic is measured on the running page: coherence is extracted along a horizontal line through the middle of the section, and the width is the number of traces whose value falls below the halfway point between the fault minimum and the background median.
