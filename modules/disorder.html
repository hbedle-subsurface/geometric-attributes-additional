Module 03  ·  Program disorder

Disorder: measuring the noise instead of the geology

Coherence is built to find edges and to treat everything else as background. Disorder is built the other way round: edges are signal, and what it hunts for is the chaotic, unpickable mess that makes a horizon hard to follow. That sounds like a small change of emphasis. It produces a completely different map, and it answers a question no other attribute here answers — how much should I trust this pick?

What is in the data

The measurement

Work through the five steps in order, then try the exercises. Method and references are there when you want them.

1Twenty-seven numbers and a stencil

A second difference in every direction at once

Take a small cube of data around the analysis point, gathered along the local dip, and multiply it point by point against a fixed set of 27 weights. That is the entire operator. The weights are the pattern 1, −2, 1 applied in all three directions, which is the standard recipe for a second difference — the thing that measures how much a set of values bends rather than how big they are or how fast they change.

A second difference is zero on anything smooth. Feed it a flat surface and it returns zero; feed it a straight ramp and it still returns zero, because a ramp does not bend. Feed it something that goes up, down, up from one sample to the next and it returns a large number. That is what "rough" means, arithmetically, and it is why this operator is blind to gently dipping reflectors and wide awake to noise.

The weights sum to zero, which is what makes the operator blind to a constant. Anything you could add uniformly to all 27 samples cancels, so the answer says nothing about whether the data are bright or dim — only about how they bend.

2Why the answer is divided by the data

Turning a number into a ratio

L·e on its own is useless, because doubling the amplitude of the data doubles it. A bright noisy reflector and a dim noisy reflector would get different scores for the same amount of roughness. So the result is divided by the length of the operator and the length of the data:

disorder = | L · e | / ( ‖L‖ ‖e‖ + ε )

Anyone who has met the dot product will recognize what that is: the cosine of the angle between the data and the operator. It runs from 0 to 1 no matter how loud the data are. Zero means the window contains nothing the operator responds to; one would mean the window is the operator's pattern, which in practice means noise of the worst kind. It is a ratio of roughness to strength, and that is a signal-to-noise measurement, which is what the attribute is for.

3The operator is only half of it

disorder, then stat3d

Run the operator at every sample and you get a volume that flickers: high at one sample, low at the next, because it is responding to individual wiggles. That volume is an intermediate result, not the attribute. AASPI hands it to a second program, stat3d, which takes its standard deviation along dip over a second window.

Taking a standard deviation rather than an average is a deliberate choice, and it is what makes the attribute mappable. An average asks how rough the data are on the whole. A standard deviation asks how inconsistently rough they are — and a package where roughness varies wildly from sample to sample is exactly a package where a horizon tracker will jump.

4Disorder is not coherence upside down

The fault is the test

Both attributes return a number near zero for well-behaved data and a larger one for trouble, so it is tempting to assume they are the same measurement with the sign flipped. Put a fault in front of them and the difference is immediate.

Neither attribute is silent at either feature, so the question worth asking is not whether each responds but which it responds to more. Coherence moves by roughly the same amount at the fault as it does in the chaotic package: it is a discontinuity detector and both are discontinuities. Disorder moves several times further in the chaotic package than at the fault, because the traces on either side of a fault are perfectly smooth — they are just at different times, and a second difference has much less to say about that than about data that genuinely bends.

That preference is the design, and it is what makes the attribute useful for the job in step 5. If you are asking which parts of a horizon you can trust, a fault is not the answer you want: your pick against a fault may be one of the best on the survey. What you want is the attribute that reserves its strongest response for data that cannot be picked at all.

Coherence and disorder share neither units nor sense, so each is measured against itself: the figures are how far the attribute moves at that feature as a percentage of its own level in the quiet part of the section. An attribute that detects something moves a long way; one that ignores it barely moves at all.

5A confidence map for a pick

The reason the attribute exists

Alistair Brown's workflow for quantifying interpretation confidence asked the interpreter to score each pick 0, 1 or 2 for reflector quality and build a map from those scores. It is a good idea that almost nobody does, because scoring every pick by hand on a modern survey is not realistic. Disorder computes the same thing volumetrically.

The panel below tracks a horizon automatically — following the strongest peak from trace to trace, the way an autopicker does — and colors the result by the disorder along it. Green is a pick you can defend. Red is a pick you should look at before you contour it, and the two are impossible to tell apart on the time-structure map alone.

Does disorder predict the bad picks?—

Every map an interpreter hands over carries an implied claim that it is equally reliable everywhere on it, and that claim is never true. Disorder is one of the few attributes whose output is about the interpretation rather than about the rock.

  • It turns a contour map into a contour map with error bars. A closure that sits entirely in a high-disorder area is a different prospect from the same closure in a quiet one, and nothing on the structure map itself says which you have.
  • It tells you where autotracking will fail before you run it. That is worth knowing in advance rather than after a day of editing.
  • It is a data-quality map, so it is also a processing map. Areas of persistently high disorder are where reprocessing, or a different migration, has something to offer. Areas of low disorder are where it does not.
  • Its preference for chaos over faults is what makes it usable for this. Coherence marks every fault as low confidence, and a pick against a fault is often one of the better picks on the survey.
  • Read it alongside coherence, never instead of it. They answer different questions and the useful thing is usually where they disagree — high coherence and high disorder together means the traces agree with each other and are all equally bad, which is a specific and treacherous situation the AASPI documentation describes on a real horizon in the Norwegian North Sea.

Try this

  1. Confirm the operator ignores a dipping reflector. Put every noise slider to zero, leave the fault throw where it is, and read the disorder in step 1 while dragging the analysis point along a strong reflector.
    HintIt stays small everywhere on the reflector, including on the steepest flanks, because a smooth dipping event has nothing for a second difference to respond to once the window is gathered along its dip. Now uncheck Compute along structural dip and watch it climb: an unsteered window cuts across the wavelet, the samples inside it go up and down, and the operator reports that as disorder. The attribute would then be mapping structure, not data quality.
  2. Watch the normalization work. In step 2, compare the raw operator output with the disorder at the same point.
    HintAmplifying the data ten times multiplies the raw number by ten and leaves the disorder unchanged to every decimal place shown. That invariance is the whole reason for dividing by ‖e‖, and it is what lets you compare disorder between a bright shallow section and a dim deep one — or between two surveys — without first balancing their amplitudes.
  3. Break the second stage. In step 3, run the stat3d window down to 1 trace and then up to 15, watching the contrast figure.
    HintAt 1 trace there is almost nothing to take a standard deviation over, so the panel is the raw speckle and the separation is close to the raw volume's. It improves quickly over the first few window lengths — most of the gain is in by about 7 traces — and then flattens: going from 11 to 15 buys very little. Meanwhile the cost keeps accruing, because a wider window puts the boundary of the chaotic package in more places at once and the edges of the anomaly blur outward. Stop where the curve flattens rather than where the number is highest.
  4. Separate the two attributes. In step 4, set the fault throw to maximum and every noise slider to zero, then reverse it.
    HintAt the settings the module opens with, coherence falls 16% at the fault and 23% in the chaotic package — it reacts to both, and almost equally. Disorder rises 23% at the fault and 133% in the chaotic package, so it reacts to the chaos about six times as strongly. Neither attribute is blind to either feature; what separates them is which one they reserve their response for, and that is enough to make them answer different questions. Note also what happens when you take the chaos away entirely: with clean data the background disorder is so close to zero that any response at all looks enormous as a percentage, which is a good reminder that a ratio against a near-zero baseline is not a measurement you should quote.
  5. Test the confidence claim. In step 5, raise the noise until the tracker starts making mistakes, then compare the two mean-disorder figures.
    HintDisorder along the mistracked traces runs well above disorder along the good ones. That gap is the entire practical claim of the attribute, and you can watch it open up as the data get worse. Note the limitation too: it tells you the pick is unreliable, not which way it is wrong, and a confident wrong pick in quiet data is something no attribute here will catch.

The main points

  • The operator is a second difference in all three directions. The weights are 1, −2, 1 applied along each axis; they sum to zero, so a constant cannot affect the answer.
  • Dividing by ‖L‖‖e‖ makes it a cosine. The result runs 0 to 1 and does not care how loud the data are, which is what makes it comparable between surveys.
  • Program disorder is one of two steps. The mappable attribute is the standard deviation of its output, taken along dip by stat3d.
  • It must be computed along dip. Without steering, the operator reports structure as disorder and the whole measurement is about the wrong thing.
  • It is relatively insensitive to faults, on purpose. A fault offsets smooth data more than it makes data rough, so disorder reserves most of its response for the chaotic zones. Coherence reacts to a fault and to chaos about equally.
  • Coherence answers "is there an edge here", disorder answers "can I pick here". Those are different questions, and the interesting places are where the two disagree.
  • Its output is a statement about the interpretation. It is a confidence map for a horizon, computed rather than scored by hand.
Method, sources, and what has been simplified

The model is a section with conformable packages above and below a middle package whose reflectors can be made to wander and whose amplitudes can be made erratic, plus an optional normal fault. Three separate difficulties can be added: band-limited random noise, steeply dipping cross-cutting events standing in for migration artifacts, and the chaotic package itself. They are separate sliders because they are separate problems and the attribute responds to them differently.

Dip is the semblance scan of the first module set. The operator is gathered along that dip with linear interpolation, and the disorder at each sample is computed exactly as |L·e| / (‖L‖‖e‖ + ε) with L the 27-point stencil. The stencil is separable — it is the outer product of [1, −2, 1] with itself three times — which is why ‖L‖ works out to 63/2 = 14.697, the figure quoted in step 1. The stat3d stage is the standard deviation of the raw disorder over a window along dip, whose length is the slider.

Where this departs from AASPI. Two dimensions rather than three, so the operator is the nine-point 2D form of the same separable stencil rather than the full 27-point one, and there is no crossline direction; the arithmetic and the behavior are identical, and step 1 draws all three planes of the true 3D operator so that what is being simplified is visible. AASPI offers rectangular and elliptical analysis windows and a choice of window size for the stat3d stage; here the window is a fixed-height strip whose width is the slider. The horizon tracker in step 5 is a simple peak-follower rather than a production autopicker, which makes it fail earlier than a good one would — the relationship it is used to demonstrate is unaffected.

The comparison in step 4 uses the energy-ratio coherence of the first module set. Both attributes there are scaled by their own range across the section before the responses are compared, because coherence and disorder have neither the same units nor the same sense, and quoting them against each other unscaled would be meaningless.
